> The defining challenge of our time is how radically diverse minds live together. Humans differ in beliefs and values, and AIs differ from us and each other. The world must hold more views than ever → **we seek to build the structures and tools that let a plurality of minds coexist and thrive, and resist the slide toward monoculture, centralised control or conflict.**
>
> _ARIA Opportunity Space: <a href="https://aria.org.uk/opportunity-spaces/trust-everything-everywhere" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Trust Everything, Everywhere</a>_

Today, in spite of technical capability compounding faster than ever, people in developed countries no longer believe the future will be better than the present: in the UK, only 14% of people surveyed said that the next generation will be better off than the present one (Edelman Trust Barometer 2026). The growing gap between what is possible and our experience of collective flourishing threatens the very idea of progress as a shared project. This is not merely a problem of perception. Flourishing in an ever-changing world requires _agency_: the ability to shape the future and adapt to change in accordance with what matters. Yet our ability to reason about where change is taking us, and to influence its direction, has not kept pace with its scale, complexity and speed. Science and technology widen the range of futures available to us; agency is how we choose the future we hope to thrive in and tailor it to meet our radically diverse needs.

Humans, as a species, have acquired agency across an unprecedented range of scales: from generating heat in individual fat cells, to putting on a coat, to building global systems for the provision of heating fuel, we combine cellular, individual and collective agency to regulate the temperatures we need. But our drive to steer what matters to us is not merely pragmatic; it is a biological imperative. We will often trade comfort for agency: people tolerate a wider range of temperatures when they perceive they have some control—a fact enshrined in building standards. Agency is both a means and an end in itself.

The problem of our time is that we have constructed a world of unfathomable peril and possibility while neglecting to build the tools we need to navigate it. Our livelihoods and prospects are increasingly coupled to decisions beyond our immediate reach and to dynamics we hardly begin to comprehend. Our innate drive for agency has run up against the relatively limited scope of our individual choices and the unintelligibility of the system they feed into. Having more choices does not give us more agency if we cannot understand what those choices mean for our futures.

Thus, our experience of agency depends more than ever on institutions—the legal and social vehicles through which we coordinate knowledge, resources and action across people, places and time—to extend our reach, represent our interests and act on our behalf. These collective capabilities support our agency when we can effectively shape their purposes and use. But while institutions ultimately derive agency from individuals (for now), they are actors with emergent agency of their own, and are subject to selection pressures: those that endure are those that secure their own resources, legitimacy and survival, whether or not that serves the people they act for. Effective alignment between our own agency and theirs is not guaranteed, and where the coupling is weak, our collective agency declines.

Technology has accelerated the growth of collective capabilities, but people's ability to shape their purposes and use has not kept pace. By their nature, institutions are better equipped than individuals to effectively wield technology—this is one reason why they're so useful—but a growing power imbalance makes alignment of agency a formidable challenge. In spite of a wealth of information, the connection between institutional decisions and people's lives remains opaque; people struggle to assess whether decisions serve them or how to improve them, and are vulnerable to manipulation and deception. Even well-intentioned institutions struggle to recognise and correct misalignment: a constituency can provide constructive feedback only to the extent the institution and its actions are legible to them. The feedback loop through which institutions can improve their alignment and become trustworthy instruments of collective agency is threatened by the very forces that make them so essential.

My ambition is to reverse the decline of collective agency and reinvigorate people's capacity to shape technological and social change towards flourishing on their own diverse terms. My hypothesis is that new technologies specifically targeting agency and accountability could help people investigate what change means for their lives, bring their knowledge to bear on institutional decisions, and strengthen the connection through which institutional agency aligns with and extends our own. Repairing this connection requires _bottom-up legibility_: ways for people to understand the wider world from the standpoint of their own circumstances, and to make that understanding consequential.

### Legibility and the epistemic commons

In _Seeing Like a State_, James C. Scott describes how institutions—in his case, states—make society _legible_: they reduce complex human systems to models that officials can read and act upon. This legibility is top-down, organised around institutional purposes such as taxation, public health or agricultural production. It is essential to institutional effectiveness and enables governance at scale. But legibility can backfire when states mistake their models for reality. Local knowledge, informal relationships, ecological diversity and unrecognised forms of value are unaccounted for, marginalised, and sometimes even erased from reality itself. Models make some things clear by making other things invisible.

Building models involves choices about what to represent and how the world is assumed to work, an exercise in purpose, values and judgement with no one answer. A map is a legible model of a territory, and there is no one map that suits every person and purpose. But technology can make shared maps useful to more people, supporting different purposes—even enabling users to enrich and correct them with local knowledge.[^1] A map will never replace local, situated knowledge, but it can progressively express more of that knowledge and remain open to correction by it.

Bottom-up legibility would put individuals[^2] at the centre of the map, making visible what matters to them: familiar landmarks and enough detail to recognise their own circumstances, alongside personalised scope to understand how those circumstances connect to the wider world. Where top-down legibility might show how a new AI-centric jobs plan could affect aggregate wages, employment, inflation and economic growth, bottom-up legibility would help a person understand what the same plan could mean for businesses in their community, their employment prospects and purchasing power, and the wider conditions that shape their wellbeing.

Individuals cannot be expected to assemble the resources and expertise needed to make society legible to them on their own, yet their agency depends on understanding beyond their immediate surroundings. Institutions, in turn, must understand the diverse circumstances of the people they represent to deserve their trust and to extend their collective agency—circumstances rich with practical, situated knowledge that they cannot adequately capture without those people's willing participation.

I believe it is now possible to build the technological infrastructure for an _epistemic commons_: a shared environment for collective inquiry where individuals and institutions bring formal expertise and lived experience together to understand the world and reason about choices that affect them. Participants could build, inspect, evaluate, contest, adapt and use models of their world, making both the representations and the reasoning they support open to scrutiny and revision. By enabling people and institutions to build on and improve each other's understanding, the commons would make bottom-up legibility possible, enrich top-down legibility, and strengthen the feedback through which institutions stay aligned with the people they serve.

Imagine a claims handler at a regional insurer, offered a place on a retraining course under a national AI transition plan as her role is automated. She is weighing it against a part-time job nearby. The plan rests on a national labour-market model, extended to represent occupations, employers and training provision by region, which projects that displaced administrative workers move into skilled trades and care within eighteen months. Using tools in the commons, she asks the model to reproduce what she has already seen: when her firm automated document processing three years ago, what became of the affected staff? The model expects most to have been retrained or redeployed within a year. But she knows what became of them: a few retrained, but more left the labour market or went part-time. Inspecting the model reveals why: it treats everyone in her occupation as equally able to take up training, wherever and however it is delivered. The nearest full-time course is forty miles away; colleagues with caring responsibilities never considered it. The tools help her distinguish workers by travel, hours and caring responsibilities, and test whether that better explains what happened. The richer representation helps her judge whether the course or a local, part-time route is realistic for her, and, if corroborated elsewhere, shows the department responsible for the plan—as well as the stewards of the national model—that its transition rates depend on how and where provision is delivered, not only on how many places it funds.[^3]

This example illustrates the reciprocal inquiry the commons would support: institutions bring technical expertise and formal knowledge expressed in models and data; individuals use tools—including AI interfaces supporting inquiry in plain language—to compose, adapt and extend these models for questions that matter to them. To be sustained, the commons must be useful to people for their own purposes, helping them reason about their own choices, with the enrichment of shared models as a by-product. The commons should become richer through ordinary use, as participants choose to contribute insights that others can scrutinise, test and reuse.

This makes institutional models _contestable_: individuals can challenge representations of their circumstances, omissions of what they value, or assumptions about choices unavailable to them. Institutions and individuals alike can use these contributions to improve their models, reasoning and decisions, while gaining a better understanding of others' viewpoints. Visible institutional responses—showing how contributions changed their reasoning, or explaining why they did not—give people a basis for judging whether their contributions matter, and whether continued trust is deserved.

The commons must earn trust by the same standard it sets for institutions. Its models, evidence and evaluations must be open to inspection and challenge, with visible provenance for assumptions and room for competing accounts of the world. Its stewardship must also be accountable: participants need to see how contributions and disputes are handled and whether challenges lead to correction. Openness enables scrutiny; justified trust also requires evidence of reliability and responsiveness.

The commons can strengthen the capacity for alignment, competence and responsiveness that already exists in our institutions, but it cannot fully close the feedback loop that aligns institutional agency with our own. It cannot ensure that every voice is heard or that institutions act on what they learn; indeed, making their shortcomings visible may give people reason to trust them less, diminishing their sense of agency. Nor can an ecology of models replace judgement: which models to use, what their omissions mean, and how to weigh competing interests remain matters for human deliberation. Even if everyone agrees on the map, they may disagree on where to go. Bottom-up legibility gives people a stronger basis for exercising agency; making institutions answerable to them—for example, through political processes—remains essential to closing the loop.

### From vision to programme, and how you can help

ARIA is often described as funding moonshots, and an epistemic commons that can restore human agency and revolutionise collective reasoning is a moonshot-scale challenge. But you don't go to the moon for the first time on a budget of ~£50m. ARIA programmes are designed to be _catalytic_: to break through an activation barrier and unlock massive latent potential. ARIA aims to support people like Robert Goddard, whose pioneering rocket research made the journey possible, rather than bankroll NASA.

My aim now is to refine this thesis and, far more challenging, to identify an ARIA-sized contribution that can catalyse a new era of individual and collective agency. The path to a plausible programme runs through the following seven questions. Below is my early thinking on each, and where I'd most value your ideas.

#### 1. What precise problem would the programme solve?

The programme would need to demonstrate the commons' essential mechanics in a bounded setting. It must show that:

1. **People will use the commons for their own purposes and can investigate what matters to them.** Affected people can frame questions about their circumstances, explore relevant consequences and alternatives, and understand how the answers depend on evidence, assumptions and value judgements—and recognise where the available models cannot answer.
2. **Different knowledge and perspectives can change the inquiry.** Participants can bring formal expertise and situated knowledge to bear on what models represent, what they omit and how they work, developing and comparing alternative accounts without requiring agreement.
3. **Participants can assess what deserves reliance.** Models and proposed revisions can be tested against evidence appropriate to their intended use, revealing supported improvements, unresolved differences and limits. Judgements about desirable outcomes remain explicit and open to challenge.
4. **The inquiry has beneficial consequences.** People can use the resulting understanding in choices available to them; institutions can use participants' contributions in their own reasoning and decisions. Contributors can see how their input was considered.
5. **The resources can support further inquiry.** Other participants can reuse and adapt the resulting models, evidence and tools for a further question, with demonstrable savings in effort or gains in capability compared with starting afresh.

Participants must be able to do all of this with realistic demands on their time, expertise and resources.

**One boundary condition:** The programme would build and test infrastructure for inquiry; ARIA would not take a position on which outcomes are desirable or take part in political processes.

_How you can help:_ Are these the right mechanics to prove? Is there an even more minimal set of mechanics that would suffice?

#### 2. Where could it change a consequential decision?

I'm looking for multiple candidate settings with consequential, recurring decisions and a plausible route to demonstrating all five mechanics within an ARIA programme's 3–5 year lifetime. Each needs an institution motivated to learn; people or local intermediaries with relevant knowledge and choices they can act on; evidence against which to assess models; and reasons to keep using and extending the shared resources. I favour settings where better understanding could deliver broad, lasting benefit through institutional reach, reuse elsewhere, or both.

Macroeconomic modelling offers one possible route: developing contestable, reusable and composable agent-based models with policy makers, central banks or private companies, with the ambition of making them central to forecasting and analysis in a manner consistent with these institutions' core missions. Explicitly representing differences between households and firms, their interactions and their adaptation could offer advantages for understanding both everyday economic dynamics and structural change.

The workforce-transition example above illustrates how this could connect to people's lives. Models of the effects of AI adoption on production, employment, leisure and incomes could provide shared components that regional institutions, businesses and communities adapt to investigate which jobs are exposed locally, where new opportunities might emerge, and what constrains people's willingness or ability to take them up. Participants' knowledge of local employers, skills and household circumstances, as well as diverse preferences for work beyond the need for remuneration, could reveal gaps in the models, with evaluated improvements feeding back into institutional analyses.

I'm not yet sure how to make the individual side of the macroeconomic setting narrowly scoped and concrete while remaining compelling. I am open to settings where the "individuals" are actually smaller-scale institutions (such as local councils). Starting with smaller institutions could demonstrate some of these mechanics, while leaving accessibility and direct value to individuals to be established.

_How you can help:_ In which institutional settings could the commons be demonstrated and used in a consequential way? What problems, exactly, would it solve, and for whom? How would success there lead to adoption in additional settings?

#### 3. What science and technology could the programme fund?

I'm exploring existing capabilities, promising research directions and barriers to use across three areas needed for people and institutions to investigate, challenge and improve models together:

- **Models that people can extend to represent their circumstances.** Heterogeneous models, including agent-based approaches, could give local constraints, relationships and differing needs an explicit place in the representation. Tools for composing models could let participants adapt and connect existing resources, facilitating the compounding epistemic base that is a core feature of the commons. The challenge is to let people make meaningful changes where that flexibility has practical value without rebuilding models or losing the meaning of their components.
- **Evaluation that keeps pace with changing models and questions.** Shared evidence, reproducible tests and formal methods could make judgements about which models deserve reliance for a given purpose more robust, explicit and repeatable. They could also help identify evidence gaps and prioritise new data collection or experiments. The biggest research gaps I've found include how to formalise judgements about suitability, uncertainty and limits; what evidence or guarantees survive modification, composition or a change of question; and where further testing or expert judgement remains necessary.
- **Interfaces that make inquiry accessible.** AI-assisted tools could help people formulate questions, find and adapt models, explore consequences and contribute challenges. AI mediation could help participants articulate perspectives, understand agreement and disagreement, and turn differences in assumptions or evidence into questions for joint inquiry. Faster simulation—for example multi-fidelity models that simulate in detail only where it matters and let learned emulators stand in for the rest—could make exploration feasible within ordinary time and resource constraints. These tools would need to support understanding and effective scrutiny, remain faithful to the underlying models and evidence, and represent participants' contributions without obscuring substantive disagreements or implying agreement where it does not exist.

_How you can help:_ Which of these three areas is the real bottleneck, and what existing capabilities, research directions or barriers am I missing? What technology is ready to apply now, and where is a breakthrough within reach? Where would we invest for maximum impact in five years? Ten years?

#### 4. How would we know it's working?

The programme has to generate evidence to show that what it develops improves on present practice and creates value for affected people. For institutions, that could mean models improved through the commons explaining and anticipating what actually happens better than the models they replace, for the uses that matter to them. For individuals, it could mean returning to the commons and making choices they judge themselves better placed to make. For the commons itself, it means demonstrable savings in effort or gains in capability through reuse, as described in mechanic 5. I need to specify a sequence of research and demonstrations rigorous enough to test the programme's core theses—and to stop the programme if they fail.

_How you can help:_ What evaluation designs have credibly shown value, both to institutions and to the people they affect, in settings like these? What metrics should the programme track and be judged by?

#### 5. What could go wrong?

How do we mitigate the risk that the programme's output becomes yet another technological arrow in the quiver of entrenched interests, further expanding institutional power at the expense of individual agency? I need to examine how misleading contributions or capture by particular interests could distort the commons, how model-dependent conclusions could be presented as authoritative facts, and how tools could create a false appearance of agreement. I also need to address the risk that information people share about their lives becomes a means of surveillance or manipulation. The programme design will need to specify how safeguards against these risks will be tested.

A central challenge is enabling people and institutions to contribute improvements while retaining control over sensitive information. I'm interested in learning more about user-controlled personal context and ways to test models where private data is held, without transferring the underlying records. The programme design will need to address how personal and private results can safely be shared in the commons while keeping model changes or improvements open to scrutiny.

_How you can help:_ What governance and guardrails could mitigate the potential for manipulation, surveillance, capture and false consensus? Are there examples where similar issues have been successfully overcome in analogous contexts?

#### 6. What is ARIA's role, and who could build this with us?

Much of the necessary capability exists in pieces: simulation engines, synthetic populations, deliberation platforms and AI that can make all of these accessible. What is unlikely to emerge on its own is the public, contestable layer that connects them to the people they affect. It is hard to fund commercially, and no single institution has the incentive to build it for others. ARIA's ideal role is to catalyse that layer—but a £50m programme need not be the whole of the effort.

Frontier AI labs are doing substantial work on AI's economic and societal consequences, including DeepMind Institute's work on agency and plural futures, Anthropic's economic scenarios and intervention research, and OpenAI's economic research and policy initiatives. These create opportunities for collaboration: their work models those futures; the commons would let affected people and institutions investigate them together, challenge and improve the models used to reason about them, and make that inquiry consequential. Philanthropies and other funders concerned with democracy, economic opportunity or AI's societal impact could likewise add funding or scope: further settings, communities or research directions that a single ARIA programme could not cover. I would love to explore shared research, demonstrations and co-funding from the outset, while preserving independent stewardship, public scrutiny and participants' control over their contributions.

_How you can help:_ If you could bring funding, data, compute, research capacity or a setting to this work—or know someone who could—I'd like to hear from you now, while the programme is still taking shape. What valuable capability could we enable together that is otherwise unlikely to emerge at sufficient scale, speed, openness or accountability? How can I best align the existing research and funding ecosystem around this challenge of collective agency?

#### 7. Where would the commons live, during and after the programme?

The programme would fund research, shared software and evaluation resources, and partnered demonstrations. But a commons needs a home as well as funding: someone to maintain shared resources, coordinate contributors, handle contributions and disputes, and provide the independent, accountable stewardship on which its trustworthiness depends. Options include supporting an existing organisation or establishing a non-profit steward, ideally taking shape during the programme so that it is ready to carry the commons forward when ARIA funding ends. The steward's responsibilities, funding and operational complexity remain to be established.

A successful programme should create a credible path to sustained use, stewardship and follow-on funding, allowing the commons to improve and expand across questions, communities and scales.

_How you can help:_ What operating structures have worked for shared infrastructure that must stay independent of its funders and largest users? Who might host, fund or partner in stewarding it, during the programme and after it concludes?

## Appendix: Additional examples of the commons

### Bottom-up views

Imagine a village shopkeeper deciding whether to expand a business serving young families. A regional planning authority's model—extending a national economic model with local detail—describes how new employers and housing could change the area's population and incomes. She uses the commons to explore what this could mean for her customer base, first checking whether the model would have anticipated changes she has already seen. The model suggests spending by more affluent families should already have grown faster than her sales indicate. Inspecting how that estimate was produced reveals that the model treats the village as a single catchment, with a uniform propensity to shop on its high street. She knows that much of the recent housing turnover has occurred at the far end of the village, where newcomers tend to shop in town or online. The tools help her distinguish these neighbourhoods and test whether accounting for their different shopping patterns better explains her sales history. If supported by further evidence, this richer representation could help her assess expansion options—and help the authority improve its estimates of how new jobs and housing translate into spending in local centres.

Imagine a parent deciding whether to have her home insulated through a regional programme. The authority's model—drawing on national energy models and local housing data—estimates how different upgrades would affect energy use and household bills. To explore what insulation could mean for her family, she first uses the commons to compare the model's estimate of her current energy use with her bills. It overestimates her heating because it assumes the whole home is kept at a comfortable temperature. She heats only the living room and briefly warms the bedrooms before her children go to bed. The tools help her represent how the family actually uses the house and explore what insulation could make possible: lower bills, more rooms they can afford to keep warm, or a combination of both. With her contribution, the model can distinguish reducing energy consumption from meeting a need previously going unmet. If corroborated across other households, this distinction could help the authority improve its demand estimates and compare which upgrades deliver the greatest improvements in affordable warmth.

### Top-down use has tremendous economic value

Imagine a central bank's forecasting team after a sharp rise in energy prices. The rate-setting committee must judge how firms will pass on costs and how households will respond, informing decisions that affect borrowers, savers and employers across the country. Yet its core model represents households and firms as a few representative types and relies on relationships estimated from quite different past episodes. In the commons, the bank's model shares components with those of regional authorities, trade bodies and households, who have spent years extending and correcting them for their own purposes. Household energy use is no longer a single demand curve: it distinguishes families who already heat only part of their homes, and so must cut other spending rather than energy consumption, from those with room to economise—the distinction the parent above helped establish, corroborated across many households and tested where their data is held. Retailers and hauliers have contributed how they reprice under different contract terms. The team composes these components with its own model in days rather than commissioning a new one, and the commons' evaluation tools show which account of household response best reproduces the spending observed after previous price rises—and where the evidence runs out, since firms' margin behaviour under a shock of this scale has few precedents. The committee receives a range with explicit reasons for its width, and can see which households and regions bear the shock hardest and how quickly relief would reach them. Because the model is inspectable, the bank can publish what it assumed, so that a household or business can find itself in the projection, challenge it, and act on it. The bank's own improvements to modelling spending under stress then become available to the authority deciding which retrofits to fund and the shopkeeper judging her customers' prospects. No institution could assemble, maintain or corroborate this representation alone; the commons lets each inherit the others' work and gives the most consequential decisions the richest picture.
